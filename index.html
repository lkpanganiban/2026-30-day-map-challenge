Day 01 · Points

133 earthquakes, two years

The Philippines sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, and the last two years made that impossible to ignore. On 30 September 2025 a magnitude 6.9 earthquake shook the Cebu–Bohol area.

Open full imageREADME.mdPython source

Output: day01_points/day01_points.png

Run: uv run python -m day01_points.map

The story

The Philippines sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, and the last two years made that impossible to ignore. On 30 September 2025 a magnitude 6.9 earthquake shook the Cebu–Bohol area. In October 2025 a magnitude 7.4 event struck off Eastern Mindanao, followed hours later by a 6.7. Then, on 8 June 2026, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake ruptured offshore of Sarangani in southern Mindanao — the strongest event in this window and the one that dominates the map.

This is the opening map of the series because points are the simplest honest shape for an earthquake: an epicentre is a location, not an area. The map does not try to show damage or intensity (that is Day 28's job). It shows how much of the country's two years was spent inside these events, and how tightly the Sarangani sequence clusters around its mainshock.

Data

  • Earthquakes: USGS FDSN Event API, starttime=2024-11-01, minmagnitude=5, bounding box 4–22°N, 114–128°E. The download is cached as data/ph_quakes.geojson.
  • Country boundary: OpenStreetMap relation for the Philippines downloaded through Nominatim/OSMnx and cached as data/ph_boundary.gpkg. It is used as a filter, not as a land silhouette, because the relation includes territorial waters.
  • Land: Natural Earth 1:10m land polygons, clipped to the Philippine bounding box and cached as data/ph_land.gpkg.
  • Licences: USGS data is public domain; OSM data is © OpenStreetMap contributors under ODbL; Natural Earth is public domain.

Process

  1. Fetch once, cache forever. The script asks USGS for every event at or above magnitude 5 in the box and writes the raw GeoJSON. Re-runs read the cache, so the map is reproducible offline.
  2. Filter to national territory. Events inside the OSM country polygon are kept with a spatial join. The raw query returns events in Malaysian and Indonesian waters; without this step the map would include shaking that is not Philippine territory. 133 events survive.
  3. Read depth. GeoPandas exposes the third coordinate as geometry.z; this becomes the depth attribute used for colour.
  4. Project. Everything is reprojected to EPSG:3857 (Web Mercator) so that distances and point sizes behave at national scale.
  5. Encode magnitude twice. Marker area scales as (mag − 4)³ × 4, and a second, much larger, very transparent copy of each marker creates the glow. Area rather than diameter carries the value, which is the correct visual encoding for a quantity like energy/magnitude.
  6. Encode depth with colour. Shallow events (under 70 km) are red; 70–300 km are orange; deeper events are blue. Shallow earthquakes are the ones that shake cities hardest, so the red should dominate the reader's attention.
  7. Label the three events a news reader would recognise. The M7.8 Sarangani, the M7.4 Eastern Mindanao and the M6.9 Cebu events get short labels with paper-coloured halos so they survive over dense clusters.
  8. Add a schematic national boundary. The dashed line is the outer limit of the OSM country relation — a reminder that this is a maritime country and that many epicentres are offshore.
  9. Compose. Dark paper background, cream type, a labelled dot legend (M5/M6/M7) and depth legend, title block top-left, sources bottom-left.

Numbers on the map

  • 133 events at M5 or above inside Philippine territory, November 2024 to November 2026.
  • Largest: M7.8, 25 km SW of Kablalan, Sarangani, 8 June 2026.
  • Other labelled events: M7.4 Eastern Mindanao (October 2025), M6.9 Cebu (30 September 2025).

Data links

External sources used for this map (the local caches live in data/):

  • USGS FDSN Event API
  • USGS Earthquake Search
  • OpenStreetMap copyright
  • Nominatim geocoder
  • Natural Earth

What this map does not say

  • USGS's M5 threshold is a catalogue choice, not a physical boundary. Thousands of smaller events are omitted, and completeness varies with distance from seismic stations.
  • An epicentre is not damage. The map has no exposure, soil, building or intensity information.
  • Depth comes from the catalogue's hypocentre solution and carries uncertainty, especially offshore.
  • The country outline is a simplified maritime/territorial relation. It is not a legal boundary and should not be read as one.

Day 02 · Lines

Nine storms entered the ring

The 2026 western North Pacific season produced nine tropical cyclones that entered the Philippine Area of Responsibility (PAR) in the IBTrACS archive used here.

Open full imageREADME.mdPython source

Output: day02_lines/day02_lines.png

Run: uv run python -m day02_lines.map

The story

The 2026 western North Pacific season produced nine tropical cyclones that entered the Philippine Area of Responsibility (PAR) in the IBTrACS archive used here. Two of them carried the season: Champi, locally named Rosal, which became the strongest cyclone of 2026 by maximum sustained wind, and Bavi, locally Inday, which left 20 people dead in July after its passage and the enhanced southwest monsoon.

The map is drawn during the week that Kalmaegi — Tino — made landfall in southern Leyte and crossed Cebu and Negros. Tino is deliberately absent from the tracks: the official best-track archive had not yet processed it when this map was made. That absence became Day 18.

Lines are the right geometry for a track because a typhoon is a path through time, not a dot. Colour along each line carries wind speed, so the reader can see where a storm intensified, where it peaked, and where it weakened.

Data

  • Tracks: IBTrACS v04r01, ibtracs.last3years.list.v04r01.csv, NOAA/NCEI, cached as data/ibtracs_last3years.csv. Each row is a 6-hourly (or 3-hourly near landfall) position with LAT, LON, USA_WIND, ISO_TIME, NAME.
  • Filtering: season 2026, named storms only, and only storms with at least one position inside a PAR test box (114.5–127°E, 4.5–22°N). Nine storms survive: Bavi, Champi, Maysak, Mekkhala, Nokaen, Noul, Peilou, Penha, Saudel.
  • Land: Natural Earth 1:10m clipped to the western Pacific (data/wp_land.gpkg).
  • Philippine boundary: OSM country relation (data/ph_boundary.gpkg), intersected with land to highlight the national territory.
  • PAR outline: a simplified polygon with vertices at 115°E/5°N, 115°E/15°N, 120°E/21°N, 120°E/25°N, 135°E/25°N, 135°E/5°N. It is schematic.

Process

  1. Load the cached IBTrACS CSV, skipping its second header row (units), and convert LAT, LON and USA_WIND to numbers.
  2. Keep named 2026 storms, then test each storm for any point inside the PAR box. This is a rough entry test, not the official PAGASA polygon.
  3. Sort each track by ISO_TIME so line segments connect in chronological order.
  4. Project to EPSG:3857 and build segment lists. A LineCollection is used rather than a single plot, because each segment needs its own colour.
  5. Colour by USA_WIND in knots: under 64 kt blue, 64–95 kt yellow, 96–136 kt orange, 137 kt and above red. A wider, very transparent duplicate of each segment adds the glow.
  6. Draw land in two tones: the wider western Pacific faint, the Philippine territory brighter, so the country reads immediately without a legend.
  7. Draw the schematic PAR boundary as a dashed line and label it once.
  8. Place each storm name at its strongest point inside the frame, with a paper-coloured halo. Bavi and Champi carry their local names (Bavi · Inday, Champi · Rosal).
  9. Add a legend of wind categories with counts and a source footer.

Numbers on the map

  • Nine named storms entered PAR in the archive.
  • Champi peaked at 159 kt, the strongest of 2026.
  • Bavi peaked at 154 kt and was linked to 20 deaths in July.
  • The track colours also show that some storms peaked well east of the Philippines before recurving.

Data links

External sources used for this map (the local caches live in data/):

  • IBTrACS product page
  • IBTrACS CSV access
  • PAGASA
  • Natural Earth
  • OpenStreetMap copyright

What this map does not say

  • IBTrACS is a best-track archive, not a real-time warning product. Its 6-hourly sampling smooths out loops, eyewall replacements and short-lived intensification.
  • The PAR outline is a simplified six-vertex polygon, not the legal PAGASA boundary.
  • "Entered PAR" is tested against a bounding box, not the official polygon, so a storm skimming the edge may be included or excluded.
  • Kalmaegi/Tino is missing because the archive lagged the event. Absence here means "not yet processed", not "did not happen".
  • Wind speeds are agency estimates; different agencies can disagree by tens of knots for the same storm.

Day 03 · Polygons

Inside the permanent danger zone

On 30 October 2026 Mayon Volcano produced a moderately explosive eruption that sent an ash column roughly 3,000 metres above the summit and forced 2,041 people to evacuate from Tabaco City, Camalig and Ligao City.

Open full imageREADME.mdPython source

Output: day03_polygons/day03_polygons.png

Run: uv run python -m day03_polygons.map

The story

On 30 October 2026 Mayon Volcano produced a moderately explosive eruption that sent an ash column roughly 3,000 metres above the summit and forced 2,041 people to evacuate from Tabaco City, Camalig and Ligao City. The 6 km Permanent Danger Zone is the line the state draws around the crater; this map asks the simplest possible question of it: what is inside?

Every building footprint that OpenStreetMap holds around Mayon is drawn as a dot. The zone is a real polygon on top of them. The result is not a hazard assessment — it is a reminder that a danger zone is a polygon, but evacuation is a list of addresses. 1,363 mapped buildings sit inside the 6 km line.

Data

  • Buildings: OSM building=* ways around Mayon, fetched from the Overpass API with out center (one point per building) for the box 13.08–13.40°N, 123.50–123.88°E. 140,694 building centres were returned and cached as data/mayon_buildings.json.
  • Roads and places: a second Overpass query returns highways (primary/secondary/tertiary) and place=city|town|village|hamlet nodes, cached as data/mayon_places.gpkg.
  • Summit: 13.2573°N, 123.6855°E, from the published Mayon coordinates.
  • Zone: a geodesic 6,000 m buffer around the summit, computed in EPSG:32651 (UTM zone 51N).
  • Event facts: 30 October 2026 eruption, 3,000 m ash column, 2,041 evacuees, from PHIVOLCS and the Albay provincial government reporting.
  • Licence: OSM data © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL.

Process

  1. Fetch building centres and context once; cache both responses.
  2. Convert the JSON elements to a GeoDataFrame with the project's small Overpass helper (osm.py).
  3. Project everything to UTM 51N so buffer distances are metres, not degrees.
  4. Buffer the summit by 6,000 m. This is a circle, not PHIVOLCS's official hazard polygon — an honest approximation made explicit in the footer.
  5. Count buildings whose centre falls inside the buffer with a spatial predicate. 1,363 of 140,694 are inside.
  6. Plot roads faintly, buildings as small slate dots, then the zone as a translucent red fill with a dashed edge and a soft outer glow.
  7. Label the towns that matter in the news story (Tabaco, Malilipot, Bacacay, Santo Domingo, Ligao, Guinobatan, Camalig, Daraga, Legazpi) with paper halos so they remain legible over dense settlement.
  8. Mark the summit with a triangle and add the zone label inside the circle.
  9. Crop the axes inside the building extract so the rectangular data boundary never appears as a hard edge.
  10. Compose the title, subtitle and source line on the series' dark paper.

Numbers on the map

  • 140,694 building footprints in the extract.
  • 1,363 inside the 6 km zone.
  • 2,041 people evacuated after the 30 October 2026 eruption.
  • Ash column approximately 3,000 m.

Data links

External sources used for this map (the local caches live in data/):

  • OpenStreetMap copyright
  • Overpass API
  • PHIVOLCS
  • Smithsonian GVP: Mayon

What this map does not say

  • OSM building completeness is uneven. The count is a lower bound on what is mapped, not a census, and dense informal settlements may be under-mapped.
  • A circular buffer is not the official PHIVOLCS hazard polygon. Real pyroclastic-flow, lahar and ashfall hazard zones are direction-dependent.
  • Building centres are points; a large building straddling the line is counted by its centre.
  • The map does not estimate how many people live in those buildings. Population would require a separate, much more uncertain dataset.

Day 04 · Clusters

Earthquakes travel in families

A big earthquake is rarely a single event. It opens a sequence: a mainshock, then aftershocks that can continue for months along the same fault.

Open full imageREADME.mdPython source

Output: day04_clusters/day04_clusters.png

Run: uv run python -m day04_clusters.map

The story

A big earthquake is rarely a single event. It opens a sequence: a mainshock, then aftershocks that can continue for months along the same fault. Over two years the Philippine catalogue contains several of these families, and they are visually obvious once the points are grouped.

The map takes the same 133 M5+ events as Day 1 and links any two that lie within 50 km of each other. The result is 27 clusters, from isolated single events to the 40-event Sarangani family that opened with the M7.8 on 8 June 2026. The strongest five families are coloured and labelled; everything smaller is left grey so the main sequences read first.

This is a deliberately naive clustering. It is spatial only — no time window, no fault geometry, no magnitude dependence. That is the point: even the simplest grouping recovers the sequences a reader remembers from the news.

Data

  • Earthquakes: the cached USGS FDSN catalogue from Day 1 (data/ph_quakes.geojson), filtered to the OSM country polygon. 133 events.
  • Land: Natural Earth 1:10m (data/ph_land.gpkg).
  • Boundary: OSM country relation (data/ph_boundary.gpkg).
  • Licences: USGS public domain; OSM ODbL; Natural Earth public domain.

Process

  1. Load the cached catalogue, read depth from the geometry's Z coordinate, and clip to the national boundary.
  2. Implement single-linkage clustering with a tiny union-find structure. For every pair of events, compute the great-circle distance with the haversine formula; if it is under 50 km, union the two.
  3. Assign each event the root of its union-find set as a cluster id. With 133 points the O(n²) loop is trivial.
  4. Sort clusters by size. The five largest (40, 20, 11, 7, 7) get fixed colours and human labels; all others are plotted as small grey dots.
  5. Build a convex hull around each named family, buffer it slightly, and fill it at very low opacity to show the footprint of the sequence without hiding the points.
  6. Plot points with magnitude-scaled area, plus a transparent larger copy for glow, exactly as in Day 1 so the series stays visually consistent.
  7. Label each family at its centroid with its name, largest magnitude and count.
  8. Project to Web Mercator, draw the national silhouette, and compose the title block and source line.

Numbers on the map

  • 133 events, 27 clusters.
  • Sarangani: 40 events around the M7.8 of 8 June 2026.
  • Moro Gulf: 20 events, largest M5.9.
  • Cebu: 11 events around the M6.9 of 30 September 2025.
  • Davao Oriental: 7 events around the M7.4 of October 2025.
  • Surigao: 7 events, largest M6.0.
  • Six events remain singletons.

Data links

External sources used for this map (the local caches live in data/):

  • USGS FDSN Event API
  • OpenStreetMap copyright
  • Natural Earth

What this map does not say

  • The 50 km threshold is a choice. A larger radius merges neighbouring sequences into a single chain; a smaller one fragments real aftershock zones.
  • There is no time constraint. Two unrelated events years apart but within 50 km are grouped as one family.
  • Clusters are not faults. They are clouds of epicentres, and epicentre uncertainty can move a point by tens of kilometres offshore.
  • The M5 catalogue is incomplete for small aftershocks, so family sizes are minima, not true aftershock counts.

Day 05 · Senses: Sight

What Landsat saw two days later

On 30 October 2026 Mayon erupted, sending an ash column roughly three kilometres above the summit and forcing more than two thousand people to leave the slopes.

Open full imageREADME.mdPython source

Output: day05_sight/day05_sight.png

Run: uv run python -m day05_sight.map

The story

On 30 October 2026 Mayon erupted, sending an ash column roughly three kilometres above the summit and forcing more than two thousand people to leave the slopes. Two days later, on 1 November, Landsat 9 passed over Albay with only about a quarter of the scene cloud-covered. The satellite saw the mountain and the ash still streaming away from it.

This is "sight" in the most literal sense: what an instrument 705 km above the ground could see. The image is a short-wave infrared composite rather than true colour, because SWIR separates fresh ash, vegetation and cloud more clearly than red-green-blue. The ash reads grey-brown, forest is green, cloud is blue. Overlaid on the image is the same 6 km Permanent Danger Zone used in Day 3.

Data

  • Scene: Landsat 9 Collection 2 Level-2 surface reflectance, LC09_L2SP_114051_20261101_02_T1, 1 November 2026, about 26% cloud.
  • Bands: swir22 (B7), swir16 (B6) and red (B4), read at native 30 m resolution from cloud-optimised GeoTIFFs.
  • Access: Microsoft Planetary Computer STAC API, with SAS signing through the planetary-computer package.
  • Crop: 123.55–123.82°E, 13.13–13.36°N, projected to UTM 51N for windowed reads.
  • Zone: 6 km buffer around the summit (13.2573°N, 123.6855°E).
  • Licence: Landsat data is NASA/USGS, public domain.

Process

  1. Search the Planetary Computer STAC for Landsat 9 scenes over Mayon between 31 October and 3 November 2026, and choose the least cloudy result.
  2. Sign the item's asset URLs, then read a window of each band. The window is computed by transforming the geographic crop into UTM 51N and using rasterio.windows.from_bounds; this avoids downloading whole 7,000-pixel scenes.
  3. Stack SWIR2, SWIR1 and red into an RGB array and apply a percentile stretch (1st to 99th) with a gamma of 0.7. The stretch makes the ash plume visible without inventing contrast.
  4. Cache the stacked array as data/mayon_landsat.npy so re-renders are instant.
  5. Plot the image with a geographic extent inside a dark figure, then overlay the danger-zone circle in two passes: a wide dark underlay for contrast and a thin red dashed line on top.
  6. Add a summit cross, a label chip, a north arrow and a 5 km scale bar.
  7. Compose the title, a three-line explanation of the colour composite, and the source footer.

Numbers on the map

  • Scene date: 1 November 2026, two days after the eruption.
  • Pixel size: 30 m.
  • Zone diameter: 12 km (6 km radius).
  • Scene cloud cover: about 26%.

Data links

External sources used for this map (the local caches live in data/):

  • Planetary Computer: Landsat C2 L2
  • USGS Landsat 9
  • USGS EarthExplorer
  • PHIVOLCS

What this map does not say

  • The grey-brown mass is interpreted as ash, but thin cloud and ash can look similar in a single SWIR composite. The map is a visual reading, not a classified plume mask.
  • No atmospheric correction beyond the provider's Level-2 surface reflectance was applied, and no plume height or mass is derived.
  • A single overpass is a snapshot. It cannot show how the plume evolved through the day.
  • The 6 km circle is a schematic buffer, not the official PHIVOLCS hazard polygon.

Mapa del Volcan Mayon

In 1814 Mayon produced one of its most destructive eruptions. Pyroclastic flows and lahars buried the town of Cagsawa; only the church bell tower survived, and it still stands as the icon of Albay.

Open full imageREADME.mdPython source

Output: day06_vintage/day06_vintage.png

Run: uv run python -m day06_vintage.map

The story

In 1814 Mayon produced one of its most destructive eruptions. Pyroclastic flows and lahars buried the town of Cagsawa; only the church bell tower survived, and it still stands as the icon of Albay. The mountain that did that is the same mountain that erupted again on 30 October 2026.

For "Vintage", the map dresses current OpenStreetMap detail in the visual language of a nineteenth-century mapa: cream paper, sepia ink, a double rule border, a serif cartouche, a compass rose and italic place names. The data is modern; the grammar is old. The result is not a historical reconstruction but a visual argument about continuity — the same cone, the same towns, the same danger.

Data

  • Buildings: 140,694 OSM building centres around Mayon (data/mayon_buildings.json).
  • Roads and towns: OSM highways and place nodes (data/mayon_places.gpkg).
  • Summit: 13.2573°N, 123.6855°E.
  • Cagsawa: 13.1647°N, 123.6947°E, marked as a cross.
  • Zone: 6 km buffer around the summit, projected to UTM 51N.
  • Event context: the 1814 eruption and the 30 October 2026 eruption.
  • Licence: OSM data © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL.

Process

  1. Load the cached OSM building centres, roads and town nodes; project to UTM 51N.
  2. Choose a paper palette (#e9dcbf paper, #4a3826 ink, #9b3a2a accent) and a serif family (Baskerville) for every label.
  3. Draw buildings as very small, low-opacity ink specks. At this scale they read as settlement texture rather than individual structures, which is the intended vintage effect.

The story

Metro Manila is a city built on a delta, crossed by the Pasig River, dozens of esteros and a dense network of drains. Data

  • Waterways: OSM waterway=river|stream|canal|drain|ditch inside the Metro Manila box (120.90–121.10°E, 14.55–14.78°N), cached as data/mm_waterways.gpkg (from Day 8's fetch).

  6. Rasterise the land polygons onto the same grid and mask out the sea.
  7. Draw the result with a five-stop colour ramp from red (0 minutes) through yellow and pale blue to dark navy (10+ minutes). Values above ten minutes are clipped into the darkest colour.

Open full imageREADME.mdPython source

Output: day24_network/day24_network.png

Run: uv run python -m day24_network.map

The story

Cebu is a long, narrow island. Its population and its primary road network run along the coast, with a mountainous spine in the middle. From Cebu City, there are two obvious ways out: north along the coast toward Daanbantayan, and south along the coast toward Santander. There is no equivalent inland alternative for most of the island.

When Typhoon Tino crossed Cebu in November 2026, the coastal road was the corridor that had to keep working. This map draws the primary network and computes the shortest path from Cebu City to each tip. The routes are almost the same length — 120 km north, 126 km south — and both hug the coast. The network is efficient and exposed at the same time.

Data

  • Roads: OSM highway=trunk|primary|secondary|tertiary ways in the Cebu box (123.3–124.3°E, 9.3–11.4°N), fetched with out geom and cached as data/cebu_roads.gpkg. 9,919 line segments.
  • Graph: built directly from the line vertices, with edges weighted by great-circle distance in UTM 51N. About 200,000 nodes.
  • Endpoints: Cebu City (123.893°E, 10.315°N), Daanbantayan (124.020°E, 11.200°N), Santander (123.420°E, 9.420°N).
  • Land: Natural Earth 1:10m (data/ph_land.gpkg).
  • Licence: OSM data © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL.

Process

  1. Load and project the Cebu road network to UTM 51N.
  2. Build a NetworkX graph from every consecutive pair of vertices in every LineString. Nodes are rounded to 0.1 m so shared OSM junctions connect.
  3. Snap the three cities to their nearest graph nodes.
  4. Compute the shortest path with Dijkstra (weight="weight") from Cebu City to each tip and sum the edge lengths.
  5. Draw the full network as thin slate lines, then overlay the north route in red and the south route in blue, each with a transparent glow.
  6. Mark the three cities with cream dots and labels.
  7. Add a legend with the route lengths, placed in the empty sea at upper left.
  8. Compose the title, the "no inland alternative" framing, and the source footer.

Numbers on the map

  • 9,919 road segments, about 200,000 graph nodes.
  • Cebu City → Daanbantayan: 120 km.
  • Cebu City → Santander: 126 km.

Data links

External sources used for this map (the local caches live in data/):

  • OpenStreetMap copyright

  • The graph connects roads where OSM vertices share coordinates. Small mapping gaps can disconnect a route or push it onto a detour.

The story

The prompt asks when something becomes a map and when it stops being one. This piece removes every conventional map element: no coastline, no labels, no legend, no scale, no north arrow, no axes. All that remains is the year's weather, drawn as ink.

Each line is a named 2026 tropical cyclone track in the western North Pacific, clipped to 100–180°E and 0–45°N. The result looks like handwriting, or roots, or a weather chart without its annotations. It is still a map in the strict sense — positions in a coordinate system — but it is also an abstract drawing. The title and subtitle leave the question open on purpose.

Data

  • Tracks: IBTrACS v04r01 last-three-years CSV (data/ibtracs_last3years.csv), season 2026, named storms only.
  • Filtering: positions between 100 and 180°E and 0 and 45°N; storms with fewer than two valid positions are dropped. 29 tracks survive.
  • Style: cream paper, near-black ink, one thin line per track, no labels.
  • Licence: IBTrACS NOAA/NCEI.

Process

  1. Load the cached IBTrACS file, keep 2026 named storms, and convert coordinates to numbers.
  2. Drop missing positions and clip each track to the western Pacific window.
  3. Sort each storm by ISO_TIME and draw a single ax.plot line at 0.45 opacity with round caps. Overlaps create darker knots where storms crossed the same water.
  4. Set the extent to 105–170°E, 2–42°N, with an aspect ratio of 1.2 (a gentle latitude correction) so the composition fills the page.
  5. Remove axes, ticks, frames and legends.
  6. Set a serif title (WIND WRITING) and an italic subtitle that states the count and asks the question.
  7. Save at 200 dpi on cream paper.

Numbers on the map

  • 29 visible storm tracks.
  • Window: 105–170°E, 2–42°N.
  • No labels, no scale, no legend.

Data links

External sources used for this map (the local caches live in data/):

  • IBTrACS product page
  • NOAA NCEI

What this map does not say

  • The count is 29, not the 86 named storms in the full basin, because tracks outside the window or with too few positions are excluded.
  • Without labels, the reader cannot identify a storm. That is the point of the piece, but it also makes it useless as a reference map.

Through the cloud, the water

In late August 2026, heavy monsoon rain and a tropical system produced floods and landslides across Luzon. Optical satellites were useless: every Sentinel-2 scene over Central Luzon in the following week was 100% cloud.

Open full imageREADME.mdPython source

Output: day26_water/day26_water.png

Run: uv run python -m day26_water.map

The story

In late August 2026, heavy monsoon rain and a tropical system produced floods and landslides across Luzon. Optical satellites were useless: every Sentinel-2 scene over Central Luzon in the following week was 100% cloud. Radar does not care about cloud.

Sentinel-1 passed over the Pampanga basin on 5 September 2026, eight days after the floods. The map shows its VV backscatter as a greyscale radar image. Water — the Pampanga River, the Candaba wetlands, the fishponds — is dark because the surface is smooth and reflects the radar away from the satellite. Cities, fields and levee banks are brighter. It is a view of what the cloud was hiding.

Data

  • Radar: ESA Sentinel-1 RTC (radiometrically terrain corrected), VV polarisation, scene S1D_IW_GRDH_1SDV_20260905T214558_20260905T214623_004448_0083EA_rtc, 5 September 2026.
  • Access: Microsoft Planetary Computer STAC, chosen from two overlapping scenes by the fraction of valid pixels covering the basin (98.6% versus 5.8%).
  • Crop: 120.45–121.00°E, 14.80–15.45°N, read at 20 m resolution into a 2,400 × 2,858 array.
  • Towns: Angeles, San Fernando, Candaba, Calumpit; Pampanga River label near 120.75°E, 14.95°N.
  • Licence: Copernicus Sentinel-1 data, free and open.

Process

  1. Search the Planetary Computer for Sentinel-1 RTC scenes over the Pampanga basin on 5 September 2026.
  2. For each candidate, read a small preview window and compute the fraction of valid (non-zero) pixels. Choose the scene with the best coverage. The first attempt picked the other scene and produced a mostly black image.
  3. Read the VV band for the full crop with rasterio windowed reads and out_shape to resample to 20 m.
  4. Convert linear power to decibels (10 × log10(VV)), clip the floor, and set zero/nodata pixels to NaN.
  5. Display with a grayscale ramp from −25 to −2 dB. Water is dark; built-up and vegetated surfaces are bright.
  6. Add town labels and a river label with dark halos so they survive the busy image.
  7. Add a vertical VV colour bar, the title, the "eight days after" context, and the source footer.

Numbers on the map

  • Scene date: 5 September 2026.
  • Resolution: 20 m display grid.
  • Coverage of the chosen scene: 98.6% of the crop.
  • Backscatter range shown: −25 to −2 dB.

Data links

External sources used for this map (the local caches live in data/):

  • Planetary Computer: Sentinel-1 RTC
  • Copernicus Data Space
  • Copernicus Sentinel-1 data guide

What this map does not say

  • The image is not a classified flood map. No threshold, change detection or ground truth was used; dark pixels are interpreted visually as water but can also be smooth surfaces, shadow, or low-return fields.
  • Eight days after the flood, some water had already drained. The map shows what remained, not the peak extent.
  • Radar backscatter depends on surface roughness, moisture and incidence angle, not only on water.
  • The scene is one moment in time. It cannot show how the flood moved.
  • No population, damage or road-closure data are overlaid.

The story

Seismographs record ground motion. People record fear. The USGS "Did You Feel It?" programme collects both: anyone can report what they felt, where, and how strong it was. For the M7.8 Sarangani earthquake of 8 June 2026, 597 responses came from 144 places, as far as 2,850 km away.

This map turns those reports into a community decimal intensity (CDI) surface. Near the epicentre the grid reaches 9.1 — violent shaking. Across Mindanao it drops to 6, 5, 4. In Luzon it is mostly 2–4: felt, but as a sway rather than a threat. The same earthquake, many feelings. That is the point of the day.

Data

  • Event: USGS event us7000srb1, M7.8, 8 June 2026, 25 km SW of Kablalan, Sarangani.

  • Licence: USGS data, public domain.

Process

  1. Query the USGS event endpoint for us7000srb1 and locate the dyfi product.
  2. Download the gridded GeoJSON from the product's content URL (the event-page URL returns HTML, not GeoJSON).
  3. The source polygons have unclosed rings, so GeoPandas refuses to read them. Parse the JSON manually, close each ring by repeating its first coordinate, and build Shapely polygons. 188 cells survive.
  4. Load the ZIP-level reports to compute the headline totals and maximum distance.
  5. Project the gridded cells to Web Mercator and plot them with a yellow-to-red ramp from CDI 1 to 10, at 80% opacity.
  6. Draw the national land silhouette beneath, then mark the epicentre with a red star and a label.
  7. Add a horizontal colour bar explaining CDI, and compose the title, the response totals and the "same quake, different feeling" line.

  • Maximum distance: 2,850 km.
  • Maximum CDI: 9.1.
  • 188 grid cells at 10 km resolution.

Data links

  • USGS DYFI programme
  • USGS event us7000srb1
  • USGS FDSN Event API

The story

Raster maps hide their own construction. A smooth hillshade or a flood mask looks continuous, but underneath it is a grid of decisions: each cell has one elevation, one class, one value. This day makes the grid visible.

The map shows Mayon's elevation surface at 30 m, with contour lines every 250 m. A zoom inset takes a 24 × 24 pixel window around the summit and draws every cell boundary. At that scale the perfect cone becomes a staircase. The 6 km danger zone is only 400 pixels across. This is the resolution at which a hazard map decides what is steep, what is high and what is safe.

Data

  • Elevation: Copernicus DEM GLO-30, tile N13_00_E123_00, cached as data/mayon_dem.npy from Day 17. 829 × 973 pixels at 30 m.
  • Crop: 123.55–123.82°E, 13.13–13.36°N.
  • Summit: 13.2573°N, 123.6855°E.
  • Zone: 6 km buffer in UTM 51N.
  • Licence: Copernicus DEM, free and open.

Process

  1. Load the cached elevation array (no new download).
  2. Display it with a custom six-stop terrain colormap from deep navy at sea level through pine and olive to cream at the summit. No hillshade is used; the point is the raw raster.
  3. Add contour lines at 250 m intervals in a faint dark ink so the cone's structure is readable without turning the map back into a hillshade.
  4. Overlay the 6 km zone as a red dashed circle and label it "400 pixels across".
  5. Compute the summit's row and column from the geographic bounds, then take a 24 × 24 pixel window centred on it.
  6. Draw the window as an inset with its own local colour stretch, a grid line at every pixel boundary, and contour lines every 100 m. The local stretch is necessary: on the global 0–2,600 m scale the summit window is almost uniform.
  7. Add a title inside the inset: "summit · 24 × 24 pixels · 720 m" and a note that each square is 30 m.
  8. Add a horizontal elevation colour bar and compose the title, the pixel-count explanation and the source footer.

  • 829 × 973 elevation pixels.
  • 30 m pixel size.
  • 6 km danger zone = about 400 pixels across.
  • Inset: 24 × 24 pixels = 720 m across.

  • Planetary Computer: Copernicus DEM GLO-30
  • Copernicus Data Space

  • Elevation is not hazard. Steepness, ash thickness, lahar channels and evacuation routes are separate datasets.
  • The DEM is a surface model with vertical error; 30 m cells smooth the summit and the ravines.
